First, facts

Most Students Study in Ways That Don't Really Work

If you've ever re-read your notes over and over, highlighted everything in your textbook, or crammed the night before an exam — you're not alone. Almost everyone starts out doing this. The problem is that these methods feel like studying, but research consistently shows they're some of the least effective ways to actually remember things.

The good news? The techniques that actually work aren't harder — they're just different. And once you understand why they work, it's a lot easier to stick with them.

Strategy How effective is it? Why
Re-reading notes ❌ Very low Creates familiarity, not memory. Feels productive but retention is minimal.
Highlighting text ❌ Very low Passive activity. Your brain isn't processing or connecting ideas.
Summarising in your own words ✓ Moderate Forces you to process information — good, but not enough on its own.
Active recall (testing yourself) ✓✓ Very high Retrieving information strengthens memory pathways dramatically.

Teaching someone else ✓✓ Very high Forces you to identify and fill gaps in your understanding.
Core Techniques

The Techniques That Actually Work

These six methods are supported by decades of cognitive science research. You don't have to use all of them — even picking two or three will make a real difference.

Active Recall

#1 Most Effective

Instead of reading your notes, close them and try to remember what you just studied. This forces your brain to actually retrieve the information — which is exactly what builds long-term memory.

How to start:

  • Read a section of your notes or textbook
  • Close everything and write down everything you can remember — without peeking
  • Check your notes and see what you missed
  • Focus your next study round on the gaps
Research by Roediger & Karpicke (2006) found that students who used active recall retained 50% more after a week compared to those who re-read.

Spaced Repetition

Memory Booster

Instead of studying one topic for 3 hours in a row, spread it out over several days. Your brain consolidates memories during sleep and rest — so gaps between study sessions actually help.

A simple schedule:

  • Study the topic on Day 1
  • Review it again on Day 3
  • Review again on Day 7
  • Final review on Day 14 (or before the exam)
This follows the "forgetting curve" discovered by Hermann Ebbinghaus — reviewing right before you forget something locks it in far more strongly.

The Feynman Technique

Deep Understanding

Named after Nobel Prize-winning physicist Richard Feynman. The idea: if you truly understand something, you should be able to explain it simply. If you can't, you don't understand it yet — and now you know exactly what to study.

  • Pick a concept you're studying
  • Explain it out loud or on paper as if teaching a 10-year-old
  • Notice where you get stuck or have to use vague language
  • Go back to your notes and fill those gaps, then try again
"If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough." — Richard Feynman

Mind Mapping

Great for Visual Learners

A mind map is a visual diagram that connects ideas around a central topic. It's especially powerful for subjects with lots of linked concepts — like science, history, or literature — because your brain remembers structure and connections, not just isolated facts.

  • Write the main topic in the centre of a blank page
  • Draw branches for each main subtopic
  • Add smaller branches for details, examples, or dates
  • Use colour, symbols, and short keywords — not full sentences
Try making your mind map from memory first, then check your notes to add what you missed. Combines mind mapping with active recall.

Flashcards & Self-Testing

Quick & Effective

Flashcards are a classic form of active recall. The key is to actually test yourself — not just read the answer. Struggle a little before flipping the card. That moment of trying to remember is where real learning happens.

  • Write a question or keyword on one side, the answer on the other
  • Look at the question side and genuinely try to answer it
  • Mark cards as "know it," "getting there," or "don't know"
  • Focus future sessions on your "don't know" pile
Apps like Anki (free) automatically schedule your flashcard reviews using spaced repetition — combining two top techniques at once.

Past Paper Practice

Exam Game-Changer

Doing past exam papers is one of the single most effective things you can do before an exam. Not just to see the format — but because answering exam-style questions under time pressure is itself a powerful memory exercise.

  • Find past papers from your school or exam board
  • Do them under timed, exam-like conditions (no notes)
  • Mark your own work honestly using the mark scheme
  • Identify your weak areas and study those specifically
Students who regularly use past papers consistently score higher — often by whole grade boundaries — compared to those who only review notes.
Focus Tool

The Pomodoro Technique — and a Timer to Use Right Now

The Pomodoro Technique was invented by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s. The idea is brilliantly simple: work with total focus for 25 minutes, then take a short break. Knowing the break is coming makes it much easier to stay focused during the work period — and the breaks prevent mental fatigue building up.

How it works

25 minutes of focused work → 5 minute break → repeat. After 4 rounds, take a longer 15–20 minute break. That's one full Pomodoro cycle. During the work period: no phone, no tabs, no distractions — just one task.

Your Study Space

Your Environment Affects How Well You Learn

Where and how you study matters almost as much as what techniques you use. Your brain picks up on context — which is why studying in the same spot, at the same time each day, makes it easier to focus. Here's what the research says about setting up an effective study environment.

Phone in Another Room

This one is hard but important. Research by the University of Texas found that just having your phone on your desk — even face-down and silent — reduces working memory and fluid intelligence. The mere presence of it causes a cognitive drain as part of your brain monitors it.

Put it in another room or in a bag. Or use "Do Not Disturb" and turn it face-down inside a drawer.

Noise Level

Complete silence works for some people. Others focus better with background sound. What consistently doesn't work: music with lyrics in a language you understand (your language brain processes it and competes with reading/writing).

Good alternatives: lo-fi music, rain sounds, white noise, instrumental music. Try Lofi Girl on YouTube or Brain.fm.

Light & Temperature

Natural light is best for alertness and mood. If studying in the evening, bright warm light keeps energy up better than dim light. A slightly cooler room temperature (around 19–21°C / 66–70°F) is associated with better focus and cognitive performance.

Dedicated Study Spot

Your brain forms associations between places and mental states. If you only study at your desk, sitting down there starts to automatically put your brain in "study mode." This is called context-dependent memory. Avoid studying in bed — it weakens both your sleep and your studying.

Stay Hydrated

Even mild dehydration (as little as 1–2% body weight in fluid loss) reduces concentration, working memory, and reaction time. Keep a glass or bottle of water at your desk and sip regularly — especially during long sessions. Hunger also impairs focus, so don't study on an empty stomach.

Block Distracting Websites

Willpower is a limited resource — don't rely on it alone. Use website-blocking tools during study sessions so you don't have to fight the temptation constantly.

Try: Cold Turkey (free, very strict), Freedom, or the StayFocusd Chrome extension. Set them up before you sit down.

🎵 What about studying with music?

The research is mixed — it depends on the person and the task. Music with lyrics consistently hurts reading comprehension and writing quality. Instrumental music or ambient sound can help some people by blocking out distracting environmental noise. The best approach: experiment and pay attention to what actually works for you.

Myth Busters

Study Myths — Busted

Some of the most common advice about studying is actually wrong. Click each myth to find out the truth.

😴 "Pulling an all-nighter before an exam is worth it."
❌ False — and this one matters a lot. Sleep is when your brain consolidates memories and moves information from short-term to long-term storage. Staying up all night means you're studying with a brain that's increasingly impaired, and then taking the exam with a brain that can't properly access what you did manage to learn. Studies consistently show that students who sleep 7–9 hours before an exam significantly outperform those who crammed all night — even when the crammers studied the same material. If you're running out of time, sleep earlier and wake up early to review instead.
🎧 "I can multitask — I'll study while watching TV."
❌ False — no one can truly multitask. What you're actually doing is rapidly switching between tasks, and each switch costs mental energy and time. When you study while watching TV or scrolling, you're processing both at a surface level and retaining neither well. Studies show that studying with background TV reduces comprehension and recall significantly. The 25 focused minutes of a Pomodoro will do more than 2 hours of half-attention studying.
📚 "Reading the chapter twice is enough to learn it."
❌ False — re-reading creates familiarity, not memory. When you re-read something, it feels familiar, which your brain can confuse with actually knowing it. This is called the "fluency illusion." You recognise the words and ideas, but if you were asked to reproduce them without the text, you'd struggle. Active recall — closing the book and trying to remember — breaks this illusion and builds real memory.
🏃 "Taking breaks is wasting study time."
❌ False — breaks are part of learning. Your brain can't sustain intense focus for hours without degrading in quality. Mental fatigue is real and measurable. Regular, short breaks actually maintain your average concentration level far better than grinding through. During breaks, your brain's "default mode network" activates — this is associated with consolidating memories and making connections between ideas. A walk, a stretch, or a few minutes outside isn't laziness. It's part of the process.
⚡ "Smart students don't need to study as much."
❌ False — and believing this is actually harmful. Carol Dweck's research on mindset shows that students who believe intelligence is fixed (a "fixed mindset") give up more easily when things are hard. Students who believe they can grow through effort (a "growth mindset") consistently improve more over time. High-performing students aren't high-performing because they're naturally smarter — they've developed effective study habits. Intelligence without strategy and effort rarely produces results.
🎯 "Studying more hours always means better results."
❌ Mostly false — quality over quantity wins. Two hours of focused, active recall studying will produce far better results than six hours of passive re-reading with distractions. Hours logged is not the goal. Actual encoding of information is the goal. That said, some subjects genuinely require a lot of time — the point is not to cut corners, but to make sure the time you do put in is actually working for you.
Exam Week

How to Handle Exam Week Without Burning Out

Exam periods are intense — but they're also manageable if you approach them with a strategy instead of just stress. Here's a realistic plan.

The Week Before: Build a Timetable

Write out all your exam dates and the time you have left. Then divide topics across the days, giving more time to weaker subjects. A written plan removes the anxiety of "I don't even know where to start."

  • List every exam and its date
  • For each subject, list the topics you need to review
  • Assign specific topics to specific days
  • Include buffer time — don't plan every single hour

The Night Before: Protect Your Sleep

The single most important thing you can do the night before an exam is sleep. Not cram. Sleep is when your brain finalises memories. Aim for 7–9 hours. Cramming at midnight is almost never worth the sleep cost.

  • Do a light, calm review of key points — no new material
  • Prepare your bag and stationery the night before
  • Set your alarm (and a backup)
  • Be in bed at a reasonable time — screens off 30 minutes before

Exam Morning: Set Yourself Up

Your mental state walking into the exam matters. Don't rush, don't skip breakfast, and avoid classmates who are panicking — anxiety is genuinely contagious.

  • Eat a real breakfast — especially protein and complex carbs
  • Do 5 minutes of calm breathing or a walk
  • Leave early enough to arrive without rushing
  • Briefly review key points — nothing heavy

In the Exam: Your Strategy

How you approach the paper itself affects your score. There's a strategy to it.

  • Read the full paper first — get an overview
  • Start with questions you feel most confident about
  • If you blank on something, move on and come back
  • Spend time proportional to marks — check mark allocations

Remember

You Don't Have to Do This Perfectly

Study techniques are tools, not rules. Start with one or two, use them consistently for a week, and see what difference they make. Don't try to overhaul everything at once — small changes applied consistently will always beat a perfect plan that never gets started.

And if you're struggling — with focus, with motivation, with anxiety about studying — that's worth talking about too. Head to the Stress & Anxiety page for support, or check out the Resources page to find someone to talk to.

💬 The most important study tip of all

Take care of your mental health first. A tired, anxious, burnt-out brain cannot learn effectively. Sleep, movement, food, rest, and human connection are not obstacles to studying — they are the foundation that makes studying possible.
