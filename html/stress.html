The main guide

Stress & anxiety, honestly explained.

Stress is not a sign that something is wrong with you — it's a sign that something matters. This page breaks down what stress and anxiety actually are, why your body reacts the way it does, and what you can do in the next five minutes to feel better.

1. What stress and anxiety actually are

Stress is your body's response to a demand. Anxiety is your body's response to a worry.

Everyone feels stressed sometimes — before a test, during a difficult conversation, or when you have too many things due at once. That's normal stress, and it can actually help you focus.

Anxiety is slightly different. It's the feeling of tension or worry about something that might happen. You can feel anxious even when nothing is wrong in the current moment — your mind is preparing for a threat that hasn't arrived yet.

Both are useful in small amounts. They only become a problem when they stay switched on for too long or are much bigger than the situation.

In one sentence: Stress is "something is happening and I need to handle it." Anxiety is "something might happen and I'm worried about it."

2. What happens in your body

Your body has an ancient alarm system. When it spots danger — real or imagined — it switches on fight-or-flight mode.

  • Your heart beats faster to pump blood to your muscles.
  • Your breathing speeds up to get more oxygen in.
  • Stress hormones (adrenaline, cortisol) are released.
  • Your muscles tense — especially shoulders, jaw, stomach.
  • Your digestion slows (that "butterflies in the stomach" feeling).
  • Your attention narrows — you might forget words or struggle to focus.

This is not a malfunction. It's your body trying to protect you. The problem is that a surprise quiz isn't actually a tiger — but your nervous system can't always tell the difference.

That's why understanding this response is powerful. Once you notice it, you can gently turn the alarm off using the techniques below.

3. Common causes for students

Most teen stress isn't one big thing — it's many small things stacking up.

School pressure

  • Upcoming tests, presentations or exams
  • Too many deadlines in one week
  • Falling behind in a subject
  • Fear of disappointing parents or teachers

Social pressure

  • Arguments with friends or feeling left out
  • Comparing yourself to people online
  • Worrying about how you look or sound
  • Group projects where things feel unfair

Personal pressure

  • Body changes and self-image
  • Figuring out who you are
  • Family conflict or changes at home
  • Sleep, nutrition, screen overload

Future worries

  • Grade requirements for next year
  • Choosing subjects or a career path
  • Fear of failure ("what if I can't?")
  • "Everyone else seems to have it figured out"
Tip: Try naming your top 3 stressors for this week. Just writing them down makes them feel smaller. You can do that on the journal page.

4. How stress shows up (symptoms)

Stress doesn't always look like crying in your room. It can be sneakier than that.

Physical signs

  • Headaches, stomach aches, or nausea
  • Tight shoulders, jaw, or neck
  • Tiredness even after sleeping
  • Trouble falling asleep or staying asleep
  • Racing heart or shallow breathing
  • Appetite changes — eating more or less

Emotional signs

  • Feeling irritable or snapping at people
  • Feeling "numb" or disconnected
  • Crying more easily than usual
  • Constant worry that won't switch off
  • Feeling hopeless or overwhelmed

Behavioural signs

  • Avoiding homework, people, or plans
  • Scrolling on your phone for hours
  • Procrastinating more than usual
  • Losing interest in things you enjoyed
  • Making more small mistakes
If any of these last more than two weeks or if you're thinking about hurting yourself, please reach out. The Resources page has helplines you can call or chat with today.

5. Practical techniques that help

These aren't magic — but they work because they talk to your body in a language it understands.

4–7–8 breathing

Breathe in through your nose for 4 seconds, hold for 7, exhale slowly through your mouth for 8. Repeat 4 times. Slower exhales signal your body that the danger is over.

5–4–3–2–1 grounding

When your thoughts are racing, pause and name:

  • 5 things you can see
  • 4 things you can touch
  • 3 things you can hear
  • 2 things you can smell
  • 1 thing you can taste

This pulls your attention out of your worries and back into the present moment.

Cold-water reset

Splash cold water on your face, or hold an ice cube for 30 seconds. The cold triggers the "dive reflex" and slows your heart rate almost instantly.

Mindful minute

Close your eyes. Focus only on your breath for 60 seconds. When a thought appears, notice it like a cloud floating past and return to the breath. You're not trying to stop thinking — you're practising noticing.

Movement break

Stress creates physical energy. Burn some of it: 20 jumping jacks, a 10-minute walk, dancing to one song. You don't need a workout — you need momentum.

Brain dump

Open the journal page and write every worry in your head without filtering. The page can hold it so your brain doesn't have to.

7. What to do in a panic moment

If your chest is tight, your heart is racing, or you feel like something terrible is about to happen — this is for right now.

  1. Name it. Say to yourself: "This is a panic feeling. It will pass. I am safe." Panic peaks in ~10 minutes, then fades.
  2. Slow your exhale. Breathe out twice as long as you breathe in. Try: in for 4, out for 8. Do this 5 times.
  3. Cool down. Cold water on your wrists or face. Step outside if you can.
  4. Ground yourself. Name 5 blue things around you. Or press your feet firmly into the floor and notice the contact.
  5. Unclench. Drop your shoulders, soften your jaw, unclench your hands. Your body leads, your mind follows.
  6. Reach out if it keeps happening. Regular panic attacks are treatable. Talk to a school counsellor or call a helpline.

8. Exam stress — before & during

Some nerves before a test are healthy. They sharpen your focus. Here's how to keep them helpful instead of overwhelming.

The week before

  • Plan study sessions in 25-minute blocks (try the Pomodoro timer).
  • Mix subjects instead of cramming one for 5 hours — your brain remembers better this way.
  • Do past papers under real time limits. The exam feels easier when the format is familiar.
  • Sleep. Your brain consolidates memory during sleep more than during a 3 AM study binge.
  • Prepare your bag, clothes and snacks the night before — fewer decisions on exam morning.

The morning of

  • Eat something (even small) — protein + complex carbs like eggs on toast or porridge.
  • Arrive a bit early. Being rushed spikes cortisol.
  • Skip last-minute panic studying — it usually makes you less confident, not more.
  • Do 4 box breaths right before entering the room.

During the exam

  • Read the whole paper first. Start with the easiest question to build momentum.
  • If you blank out, skip the question, breathe, come back. Blanking is normal — it's not "over."
  • If you feel panic rising: put down your pen, breathe out slowly for 8 seconds, press your feet into the floor, keep going.
  • Don't compare your pace to others. People finish early for all kinds of reasons — it doesn't mean they did better.
Reality check: one test does not decide your future. You can retake things. You can change your mind. You matter more than any grade.

9. Healthy habits that make stress smaller

The goal isn't to become a perfect wellness guru. It's to stack tiny habits that keep your baseline calm.

Sleep

Teens need ~8–10 hours. Phones in another room, dim lights 30 minutes before bed, same bedtime on weekdays. Missing sleep raises stress hormones the next day.

Food

Eat regularly, especially breakfast. Low blood sugar feels like anxiety. Water matters more than you think — mild dehydration lowers concentration.

Movement

20–30 minutes of walking, cycling, dancing or sport most days. You don't need a gym. Movement is the best-studied way to regulate mood.

Screens

Set a stop-time for social apps. Endless scrolling before bed wrecks sleep and feeds comparison. Keep one screen-free hour a day.

Connection

Spend real time with at least one person who feels safe — a friend, parent, sibling, grandparent. Isolation makes stress louder.

Down-time

Your brain needs boredom. Let yourself do nothing sometimes — stare out a window, draw, walk with no destination. That's when ideas arrive.

10. When and how to ask for help

Asking for help is brave, not weak. Absolutely nothing you feel is too small to talk about.

Reach out when…

  • You feel sad, anxious or overwhelmed for more than two weeks.
  • You're avoiding school or things you used to like.
  • You're thinking about hurting yourself, or feel like you don't want to be here.
  • You don't know why you feel the way you feel — that's a great reason to talk.

How to actually say it

You don't need a speech. Try one of these:

  • "Can I talk to you about something? I've been feeling really stressed lately."
  • "I don't know exactly what's wrong, but I'm not okay and I'd like to talk."
  • "I need help with something. Can we sit down later?"

Who to tell: a parent, older sibling, relative, school counsellor, favourite teacher, coach, or a helpline. Pick the person who feels safest — it doesn't need to be the "right" one.

Helplines (Austria):
  • Rat auf Draht — for children & teens. Free, anonymous, 24/7. Call 147 or chat on rataufdraht.at.
  • Telefonseelsorge — emotional support. Call 142, any time.
  • Emergency (any situation): call 112.
